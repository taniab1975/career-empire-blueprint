## A believable world, with a sense of possibility

**Cinematic stylised realism**

Current amendment: ECC Career Campus + Campus-to-Career Corridor

Career Empire should feel like a place a young person wants to step into: recognisable, welcoming and full of possibility. Its people have warmth and personality; its buildings have substance, purpose and life inside them. Contemporary architecture, rich planting, natural materials and carefully placed technology create an optimistic near-future town. The camera brings us close enough to care. Light invites us towards an opportunity; movement makes a discovery feel earned. The world can struggle, recover and flourish without losing its identity. The ambition is the depth, craft and emotional presence of the supplied ElevenLabs Town Hall films, translated into a coherent, playable 3D world.

## The 3D translation

Rebuild the selected designs as spatially believable, walkable 3D environments. Do not simply place the existing isometric pictures on planes, rotate a flat facade, or keep white-background display platforms. A generated 3D-looking image or film is a visual reference, not a mesh, rig or finished playable asset.

## Art direction standards

### Modern by default; distinctive by purpose

Use the approved ECC campus and pavilion family: warm limestone/pale brick, navy framing, timber fins, generous glass, verandas and layered native planting. Historic Town Hall art is retained comparison, not the everyday architecture target. Match material quality and human scale without duplicating every roof.

### Detailed and tactile, not photographically literal

Use convincing stone, glass, cloth, timber, water and foliage with softened, deliberately designed forms. Keep contact shadows, consistent scale and readable silhouettes. Avoid low-detail clay placeholders as final art, shiny plastic surfaces, photographic people in a stylised town, and excessive micro-detail that disappears at play distance.

### Warm people, teen-appropriate presence

Characters should convey curiosity, concern, confidence and recovery through expression and body language. Preserve identity from shot to shot and between studio and world. Translate the supplied pose-sheet warmth into natural 3D movement without making every character constantly smile or cheer. Do not silently replace the existing explorer's jacket with a blazer; approve wardrobe by character and context. Broaden representation through approved character designs, not random regeneration.

### Nature belongs to the town

Use layered planting, varied grass, garden edges, water and places to pause. Integrate paths, ramps, crossings and bridges into continuous terrain. Retain the crafted richness of the selected 2D environment references, but remove their isolated tile presentation. A place should look explorable from ground level as well as understandable from above.

### Light reveals opportunity

Use clear daylight for ordinary exploration and warm interiors against cooler exteriors for selected evening scenes. Maintain visible doors, faces and route surfaces. Cyan or teal marks useful technology and interaction; warm light suggests occupancy. Do not make every view dark blue, coat every edge in neon, or let bloom hide architecture. Night scenes are a lighting variant, not the universal palette.

### Three related visual layers

The town is grounded and spacious. Learning tools and displays may use more focused technology effects. Rewards may briefly use stronger green, gold, cyan or violet celebration cues. Keep them recognisably related without putting a glowing reward-frame around the entire world. Colour is accompanied by symbols and readable text; the reward artwork does not determine game economics.

### The camera brings the player into the experience

Use an aerial view to understand the town, then character-relative perspective to approach a place. Keep the destination readable, show a purposeful reaction where useful, and cross a believable threshold into a consistent interior. The supplied clips begin at ground level; the aerial-to-shoulder connection comes from earlier project guidance. Prefer steady movement over whip pans, excessive blur or unexplained cuts. Cinematics need a skip/reduced-motion alternative in the eventual game.

### Development changes care and activity, not identity

Disrepair, Growth and Flourishing should retain the same underlying architecture, route graph and character proportions. Show weathering and reduced activity, then repair, then maintenance and life. A disruption is a separate temporary layer and personal progression is separate again. Do not turn disadvantage into ugliness or make a crisis erase all visual evidence of earned progress.

## Avoid

- Flat isometric cut-outs presented as finished 3D conversion; single textured facades used for unrestricted orbit.
- A uniform town of old-fashioned civic buildings, or an equally uniform town of glass boxes.
- A constantly dark neon city, permanent portal spectacle, floating holograms without purpose or strong bloom over every edge.
- Miniature white display islands, oversized toy proportions, blank interiors, floating feet and doors that cannot be approached.
- Uncontrolled changes to faces, hair, uniforms, badges, building footprints, doorway locations or time of day between shots.
- Baked-in generated text, invented school crests, copied cultural motifs, arbitrary reward values or new gameplay mechanics hidden inside an art brief.

## From reference to production

1. Choose a named reference for each job: architecture, character identity, material/lighting and camera. Attach the actual files; filenames in a prompt alone do not give a generator access to them.
2. Write the asset's purpose and intended experience. Lock identity, silhouette, dimensions/scale, entrance position, materials, lighting and exclusions before requesting variations.
3. Reuse the approved source set first. Develop the dimensioned approach and modular kit in the separate candidate; additional reference generation or vendor spending requires explicit scope and approval.
4. Approve one reference set, then build or commission the actual mesh, materials, rig and animation. Reuse the same geometry for states and camera shots; do not generate a different building for every frame.
5. Compare a rendered film reference with a real-time engine test on target school hardware. Agree measurable asset, lighting and performance budgets before production; no frame-rate or film-quality promise is made by this guide.
6. Record source, prompt, tool/model/version where available, date, rights, parent references, output, approval status and runtime test evidence. Keep original references and editable source aligned with the live Blueprint record.
